*It\'s late 1879 and the baking Australian summer has just started. George Ernest Morrison had decided to walk from his home in Geelong to Adelaide. By himself. His planned coastal route would be some 750 miles (1200 km). He was 17. And he kept a diary.*

# The Diary

## December 1879

##### Tuesday December 30th 1880 \[sic\]

This morning having made all preparations beforehand I started for a walk to wile away my holidays to I don\'t know exactly where but at any rate as far round to the coast as practicable.

All necessaries are carried in a canvas knapsack on my back, for defence a tomahawk in my belt while cooking utensile in the shape of two billies suspended by straps dangle behind.

A sympathising friend wishing to see me fairly on my trip accompanies me to the Barwon Heads. A fisherman puts me across the river and I land at the Sheepwash a sandy beach two miless from the mouth. I start to take a short cut across country to Barwon Creek but the crushing weight of my Knapsackk told against me and I failed to go the distance. Stoppages for rest become more frequent till at length I decide to camp. I choose for my bed a corner of a paddock protected on two sides by a thorny hedge.

##### December 31st 

My slumbers last night were disturbed by dreams of snakes and octopuses. I set off for Barwon Creek soon after daybreak and walking through I witnessed the phenomenon (to me) of the sunrising. Barwon Creek is very uninteresting as is also Spring Creek six miles further on. The walk between the latter and Swampy Creek (a distance of two miles or so) is really worth taking for scenery alone. The cliffs are magnificant for me to attempt to describe them would be presumption indeed. The variety is bewildering. Some are yellow, rough and ragged with no trees near them, some are perfectly perpendicular with the [\[indecipherable\]]{.small} of formation horizozntal perpendicular or vertical. In some places the cliffs rise tier above tier bare in front but woodedon the summit to an immense height while in other places the cliff is perpendicular and bare for a hundred feet or so and then gradually shelves in a splendidly wooded incline down to the sea beach.

My bed at Swampy Creek is in a clump of trees beside the river and lying awake with all sorts of queer noises going on around me I see the Old year out and the New Year in.

## January 1880

##### January 1st 1880. 

Fine day. On getting up this morning I found I was wet through and very sore, my feet also were raw and in no fit state did O feel for travelling. From the hill overlooking Swampy Creek a varied and extensive view of wooded ranges and water was obtained. I took tho old track near the coast to Ayrey\'s Inlet as my wish is to keep within sight of the sea as much as possible. The Inlet so called is a low lying plain four or five miles long by half a mild broad, protected on three sides by high hills and separated from the sea by a low sea bank. Serpentining through this plain in the most extraordinary manneris Asprey\'s Inlet creek. After passing a creek five miles from the inlet I took to the hills and let the telegraph line be my guide to Reedy Creek. I got into Mountjoy\'s at Lorne in time for New Year\'s dinner and must acknowledge found goose and plum pudding a pleasant change to cold mutton and dry bread. In the evening I was shown over the Grand Pacific hotel which is magnificantly fitted up and cost £ 13000 £ (it is said). Slept in an old bathing shed.

##### January 2nd 

Fine. Up early. Left about 10 A.M. and a Mountjoy told me I could easily walk to Apollo Bay before Sundown only took two small packages of sandwiches with me. After one continuous [\[indecipherable\]]{.small} of jumps for four miles I came to Cumberland Creek, a well known beauty spot. I naturally am rather fond of jumping from rock to rock when unburdened and in good condition but my knapsack effectively jolted my wind out and with my wind away flew all sense of enjoyment. Arduous climbing for four miles over still higher rocks round the base of huge Mt. Defiance brought me to Jamieson\'s river very similar in its picturesqueness to the Cumberland and four miles further on I came to the top still more beautiful. Stoppping here I found two sportsmen on a fishing excursion and as it was later in the day and I was very leg weary I resolved to camp with them. We made a miamia and before turning in lit a glorious fire at out feet. The night was cold and I alone slept.

##### January 3rd. 

Another fine day. Had breakfast early and started soon after for Apollo Bay as we had a long walk before us. Four miles uninteresting walking over hard but smooth rocks brought us to the Kennet a fine large salt creek barred at the mouth. We still kept the track for two miles more till we came to Cape Paton. This Cape is always guarded by the sea and \"The Paton\" is the high hill which has to be crossed ~~in cutting~~ to avoid going round the cape. A well beaten path running along the line of telegraph led us up the hill in one continuous clims for an hour or so, then one of my companions suggested we should turn away to the left and get on to the shore again. Accordingly we struck off and worked our way through dense scrurb to the high range overlooking the Sea and by keeping along the top of this range we hit a track which took us to the back.

From where we were to Apollo Bay is said to be a walk of ten miles though after eallking it we were all agreed that seventeen miles would be a more truthful estimate and in this distance we passed thirteen lovely little creeks of freshwater draining the ranges. The hills though always very similar in their appearance never tire the eye nor become monotonous.\
The township of Apollo Bay which we reached about tea time consists of a telegraph office and Cawood\'s Accommodation house. There arrived here an hour before us two well known Melbourne gentlemen on a pleasant excursion who travel with more ease and comfort then I do as a packhorse carries all sorts of luxuries. Today I had rheumatic pains all down my back brought on by sleeping out without any covering so in future I am going to make it a rule never to sleep out when a house is within ten miles.

##### January 4th. 

Fine day. Was delighted to find this morning that I was to have the company of the two tourists mentioned a bove as far as the Parker. After some delay in saddling the packhorse we set off and once started we got over the ground in good style. A well defined track led us through the forest to Blanket Bay. On the top of a hill we spent half a hour in dislosdging a snake from a hollow log we had seen it slip into. From Blanket Bay we kept a well beaten dray path to the Parker a beautiful river with a rustic bridge, distant four miles from Cape Ottway and thirteen from Apollo Bay. Here I left my companions and went on alone. I got to the Cape just before dark and was most cordially welcomed in by Mr Kelsall who has charge of the telegraph station. In the distance his house looks like a cathedral. Cape Otway as of course everybody knows is the southernmost point in Australia.

##### January 5th. 

In the morning I was shown the splendid caves and beautiful limestone grottoes and went up the lighthouse, this is a round stumpy looking structure with walls five feet thick. The light is a revolving light of the first order transmitting a flash once every minute to a distance of twenty four miles. There are twenty one lamps and it takes seven lamps to make a flash.

After lunch I started for a short walk to a hut, the owner of which was to direct me to the Gillibrand. I followed the coast line for three or four miles till I came out on the top of a high seabank overlooking an immense bay of bright sand. A silvery thread of water glistened under the lofty bank at the other side. This was the river Aire and as it was only three feet deep I had no difficulty in getting across. The hut I was making for is situated in the valley of the Ford a tribuitary of the Aire so easily found it.

##### January 8th 

After a good rest rose early, On leaving my directions were \"After crossing the Johanna previous to which you will cross a small creek you will see a path going up the face of the hill. It will take you to the Gillibrand.\" I got on the to the track easily enough. It was just a narrow footpath eighteen inches wide and as it had only once been crossed in the previous twelve months I couldn\'t expect it to be very clearly defined. It kept along the edge of the cliffs for a while but then to my astonishment it turned away at right angles into the forest evidently on the top of a dividing range. The path wound about in the forest in the most puzzling manner though I fancied it was gradually making for the sea again. At length I knew I was getting near the sea coast from the change in the flora and shortly I got on to the top of a hill from where I could see the sea. I then lost the path but as I knew the direction I was alright. By keeping round the base of the hills I eventually emerged into the open on the bald hill slope. I was very thirsty as I\'d had nothing to drink since leaving the Johanna.

I took a short rest and then hurried on towards a sandy hill I saw in the distance and as usually mark the mouth of a river. Going there to my great relief I came across an empty house with a half-filled barrell of rusty water at the back. Ten hours is a long time to go without water walking all the time and I suffered accordingly. Just near here is Moonlight Head where the Maria Gabriel was wrecked. The Gellibrand was before me though only part was visible because of the high hills. I went down to the mouth and lay down on the sand till dark. The vein with the river lying placed before me, the low sea bank on my left and huge Pt. Ronald opposite casting a dark shadow on the water and just cutting off half of the setting sun was charming. When it was fairly dark I went exploring and discovered a boat with oars and everything in it apparently left expressly for me. I was across the river and had it tied up at the other side in a very short time and a couple of hours afterwards was admitted into Glenample fairly done up. My walk today was thirty five miles. Glenample H.S. is four miles from the Gellibrand and forty three from Cape Otway.

The next eight days I spent in seeing scenery and in getting my feet well for from the unaccustomed hard work they had festered in several places, The cliffs all along the coast near Glenample are perfectly perpendicular of a yellow color much broken with gorges, This coast is famous for the wreck of the Loch Ard which took place early in the morning of Saturday June 1st 1878, the gorge in which it struck is so broad as to seem to run but a short distance into the land, In the entrance to this gorge with part inside but the grater position outside stands a long narrow detached cliff with a tunnel running through it parallel to the coast. At the Eastern end of this tunnell on the ledge of rock which forms the pedistal of the cliff itself the Loch Ard stuck. The gorge into which Pearce and Miss Carmichael were washed is the next one to the West and it is a remarkable fact that this is the only gorge for miles into which a human being could be washed with safety in a heavy sea. The entrance to it is narrow but the gorge gradually widens till it is met by a headland running out from the land which divides it into two other gorges, the westerly one being ever so much the longer and deeper.

At the far end of each of these there is a cave containing fresh water, The cave in which Tom Pearce put Miss Carmichael is just round the first corner on the left hand side as you come in from the sea. It runs straight out to sea again for a distance of seventy five yards. Each of the subgorges is covered with stunted bushes. The fine broad beach is accessible by steps cut down the face of the headalnd into the Western gorge and at the foot of these steps is where the tree Miss Carmichael hid herself under when she heard the \"cooees\". One of the most interesting sights about here is the \'blowhole\' an immense chasm sixty yards long, ten to thirty broad and as near as I could tell fifty feet deep, distant over a hundred yards from the sea but holding communication with it by a huge subterranean tunnel. On the slope of the hill near it is the \'Loch Ard\' graveyard where Reginald Jones, George Arthur Townshend Mitchell, Mrs Carmichael and Miss Ruby Carmichael, the only four bodies distinguishable after recovery from the wreck are buried.

##### Thursday Jan 15th 

My feet having quite healed I continued my journey, the Sherbrooke is four miles from Glenample and though a large river is not at all picturesque. Having crossed by the usual sandbar I had to climb an exceptionally high hill of loose sand before getting onto the track again. The scenery consisted of a bald hill slope with a couple of creeks to give variety.

Port Campbell is a broad bay girt with high hills and runs a long way into the land The water in it is very deep and there is excellent anchorage for the largest ships It is not however a safe port being exposed to all Southerly and Southwesterly winds and there is also some difficulty in getting into it as a chain of rocks runs in a slanting direction from the eastern side of the mouth across the entrance. A low seabank lies between the post and the valley of the Port Campbell creek which runs away between high hills out of sight. On the Western shore was still visible the hull of the \'Napier\' a small steamer wrecked while bringing goods from the Lock Ard and one of its boats was lying on the beach. A man was making preparations for a voyage round to Geeelong in this boat alone.

On leaving the Port I kept along the edge of the cliffs for some time till the walk became so laborious that I cut away to the Northwest and fell in with the road again. The country for five miles to my right was diversified with the stumps of trees that surrounded the springs, to my left were bald hills covered with stunted bushes. Then the country became more thickly timbered and wattles and the sheoak made their appearance. A gorge close beside a fence here was the finest I had yet seen. From the appearance of the coast I inferred I was getting near some large river (I had no map with me).~~getting near some larger river,~~ Sandy hills having taken the place of previous bald ones and shortly I came in sight of an immense sheet of water stretching away to the north as far as you could see and seemingly two or three miles broad. Swans were swimming about in it close to the shore in hundreds.

This was Curdies Inlet and I was very much astonished to find its mouth barred with a strip of sand which separated the inlet from the bay formed by the land and a spit of sand which separated the inlet from the bay formed by the land as a spit of sand running out from the Eastern shore. This bay is very shallow abounds with rocks and this is utterly unnavigable. On the beach lies the wreck of the \"Young Australian\" once the \"Carl\" of Dr Murray, Mount and Moprris, and the South Sea Islanders Kidnapping notoriety. At the end of the spit of sand is the \"Schomburgh rock\" which marks the spot where the \"Schomburgh\" was lost. The distance from Curdies\' Inlet to Glenample is 15 miles and to Port Campbell seven miles. I put up at a house belonging to a Mrs Robertson and found it densely populated with a very well grown powerful species of the Pulex irritaus [\[transcribers note: fleas\]]{.small}

##### January 16th 

Leaving about 10 AM. I still kept on the road and my attention was first drawn to a deep bay with little gorges running out from it into the land all round like pendants from a necklace. The cliffs are low out at the summit of sandstone formation jagged in the front and the majority are underlined. Near the road the country is barren and undulating but away to the North could be seen forrests of rung timbers. At intervals water has collected in the hollows of the gorges and these become overgrown with weeds. Walking along you catch a glimpse every now and then of the inhospitable coast always the same water washed cliffs, with the huge detached rocks and sea dashing round them.

I had lunch with a Mr Wm Irvine of Nerranda a genius, a poet, painter and phrenologist and the monitor of a new system of phonetic spelling, but before sitting down I went across to the \'Bay of Islands\'. There is nothing very remarkable in this. Just an inlet studded with lofty detached cliffs standing in a sort of line, the centre one of all being called \"Lot\'s Wife\".

A walk of 10 miles through an uninteresting country tenanted chiefly by rabbits and these were in countless thousands brought me to a slygrog shanty from where I was directed to the house I was bound for. To the owners I presented a letter of introduction and though it was a very busy time reaping being in full swing I was most cordially welcomed in. The house is distant eighteen miles from Curdies\' Inlet and twelve from Irvine\'s

##### January 17th 

It seemed as if it were going to rain this morning when I started to walk the twelve miles to Warrnambool but it turned out a fine day. The house I spent the night in is some distance from the sea though quite close to the main road. I learn that it is shorter to keep near the coast so gradually edge across the country towards it in doing which I have to cross fields of stubble and jump any number of fences. The swampy tract of country near me gradually merges into a large lake whose name I have forgotten. I get onto the line of hills near the head of the lake and between it and the sea and walk along their ridge (there was no track) for eight or nine miles. The coast hills become lower and lower till they seem at Warrnambool in the distance to run down into the sea. When two or three miles from Warranambool I turn down to the right and strike the mainroad. The Hopkins is a magnificant river, nearly two hundred yards broad and runs within a mile and a half of the very centre of the town. Warrnambool is a lively looking place with cabs and very fine hotels. My unusual garb excites astonishment in the minds of the people and a feeling that I am ridiculous in myself so I lose no time in allaying both.

I am again on the road this time bound for the house of a gentleman ~~who I was told lived near Town Hill~~ living somewhere near Town Hill Lake. Walking on a hard metal road as I am is very tiring, uninteresting and irritating. Mr Rutleldge\'s station is on both sides of me, splendidly grassed and watered. About seven miles from Warrnambool I leave the metal path and cut across fields to a lofly hill with a telegraph line on the top. From the top of this a curious view met my eyes. Below me was a lake about 6 miles round and almost circular with a lofty range of hills sloping abruptly down for the bank all round except in one part where the banks were low. In the middle of this lake in fact taking up almost all but a narrow strip round was a curious long formation of mountains forming an island. This mountain is called the Town Hill Mount and the lake is the Town Hill Lake. The water is covered with weeds there being only one bare patch and that was opposite the low banked side of the hill.

About a mile round on the top of the hill from the lake I saw a large stone house. This was my destination and very glad was I to get there.

##### January 18th 

Sunday. Town Hill from where I spent the night consists of three hundred and twenty acres of the finest land in Victoria and is distant twelve miles from both Warrnambool and Belfast. The back of the house looks south over the lake, the front takes in all the farm, the township of Koroit and the forest in the background.

As it was Sunday I took a good rest. Leaving about 2 P.M. I kept round the lake till I got onto a road which took me to the low sandy coast hills. In this walk I passed through the village of Crossley and had to undergo much jeering from a hoard of gaping rustics. I corssed over to the back and walked barefoot to within a short distance of Belfast and as the sea was out and the beach was fine and hard I had a pleasant brisk walk. When near a landmark I came over the sandridge again and followed the main road into Belfast crossing by a bridge the river Moyne. I put up at the Caledonian Inn. Belfast is a dull wretched place. The river Moyne before entering the sea divides into two branches enclosing an island. Small vessels can be navigated up the Eastern branch right into the centre of town. At the extremity of the island is a red lighthouse.

##### January 19th 

Left early. The the mainroad to Lowbuk a distance of twelve miles. I was within a mile or two of the sea all the time. A great quantity of ferns were about also rung gumtrees. Lying dead in the middle of the road I passed a snake about three and a half feet long of a dirty black color banded with light yellow. Would it be a tiger snake? Lowbuk is a small struggling bush township with a hotel or two, a couple of stores, a church, schoolhouse and postoffice.

I kept the mainroad for two miles and a half after leaving Lowbuk but then I had been told there was a shorter road near the coast I turned left to get on this road. En route I crossed the Eumerella by a wooden bridge with stone abbutments!!! This river is hardly distinguishable owing to the thick overgrowth of rushes, in fact the only clear patch to be seen was just under the bridge. It drains a sort of extensive marsh crossed with coarse grass and thistles and its water is drinkable.

I had been for some time steadily making my way towards the coast and when I got on to a high ridge I came in full view of the sea. To the S.E. was the lofty flat topped \"Julia Percy\" Island away to the S.W. I could just distinguish the Lawrence rocks at the extremity of the land. The road led along the top of this ridge fro seven or eight miles. On both sides of it were low marshy valleys, the one on the right being between it and another line of hills, the one on the left separating it from the sandy seabank. The ridge gradually became flush with the plain and then I left it, crossed over to my left and got on to an old track at the base of the sand hummocks. I found this too heavy walking so I cut over on to the beach. The tide was out and the sand was hard consequently I rattled along for a long time. Wondering why I saw no signs of the Fitzroy River I crossed the sea ridge once more and again got onto the track. This I followed till I came to the Fitzroy.

This river at its mouth could be mistaken for a narrow arm of the sea running parallel to the shore on the sea side of the hummocks. The other bank was pure white sand with only one or two trees on it. Crossing a small tributary I turned up inland to make enquiries at a house I saw. Having received directions I started away at 7 P.M. for the only house of accommodation near distant four miles off. By keeping due north I fell in with the coach track and telegraph line between Portland and ~~and the mouth of the Glenelg~~ Belfast just as it was getting dark. In this walk I disturbed ten or twelve Kangaroos I got to the house kept by one Brownlow a little after 8 P.M.

##### January 20th 

In the middle of last night I was awakened by somebody driving furiously up to the house, shouting out \"House! there! House! Well you are a fine lot of people and this he kept repeating. Mrs Brownlow screamed out with her shrill voice from her room \"What the ~~devil~~ do you want\". It turned out that the man wanted some matches. But for this and another time when the owner of the house had some gun practice at a native cat in the tree which shaded the window of my room, I slept well.

After a good breakfast I left about 7 A.M. My walk yesterday was 31 miles.

The day promised to be a regular scorcher. Somehow or other I didn\'t feel at all in good trim for walking and every few minutes had to take a rest. The country was well timbered and pretty. About two miles and a half from Brownlows is the Darrow a swift beautiful clear stream which glides along perfectly noiselessly. The banks are low and covered with scotch thistles.

Shortly after crossing the Darrow bridge I came to another bridge. This was over a chain of waterholes which form the early stages of the Fitzroy river. The last post town was Tyrendarra.

I still walked on though the heat was intense and the hot wind scortching. (I have sice learnt the the temperature on the road was 115° Fahr in the shade). I called in at a house to see if I could get some fruit and chummed in with the owner an old digger Frederick Saunders who landed in Portland so long ago as beginning of 1837. I started away again about 3 P.M. to walk the fourteen miles into Portland and it was a painful trudge. The forest extended for for another mile of the road and then there was a distance of four miles of perfectly treeless flats to the Surrey River. So utterly devoid was the country of any tree large enough to give shelter that I had to sit down under the shade of a telegraph pole to cool my head for the only protection from the sun I had was a heavy peaked wollen winter cap. The Surrey is nine miles from Portland and has a most extraordinary course. From the bridge it was seen to flow first almost due East and then to turn round and flow almost West till it was brought up on the beach by a sandbar. The water is salt and the banks are low and marshy. Just near the bridge and close the water\'s edge is a spring of splendid water.

I took the beach followed it for seven miles, then got on to the road, lost it in the dark, blundered up a high hill alongside the telegraph line, got on to the road again and arrived in Portland very tired about 10.30 P.M. As it was so late I turned in at the first hotel I came to.

##### January 21st. 

The hotel I spent last night in is called the Lamb Inn Kept by S. McConachy and has the reputation of being the worst managed, the dirtiest and the filthiest of all the hotels in Portland and it has justly earned this reputation. I had nothing to eat before going to bed I was so tired. Hunger, vermin, heat and bad ventilation prevented me sleeping and to add to all my misery I wasn\'t called this morning till breakfast was finished so all I got was some cold fish, recooked old bread and rancid butter.

Before leaving for Cape Bridgewater I strolled about the town but Portland is so well known being connected by railway with Melbourne that I had better not say anything about it.

Today was pleasantly cool. To get to Bridgewater you have to follow the road between Portland and the mouth of the Glenelg for five and a half miles with the customary Australian bush scenery on both sides of you and then turn away to the left. The road now becomes one of very heavy sand which of course adds proportionately to the fatigue of walking and has many branches but they converge.

One stream of running water is crossed by a bridge and soon after you come within sight of the seacliffs and kept within sight henceforth. The country gradually becomes more open and a great part of it is under cultivation though the crops didn\'t look much.

Cape Bridgewater is a bold headland running in a South Easterly direction far out into the sea and forming the Western boundary of a deep bay, the other Cape opposite being Cape Nelson.

McKinley\'s house for accommodating visitors is situated on the Eastern slope of this high headland looking towards Cape Nelson. Below is a rocky track but at the head of the bay is a long strip of beautiful sand. A branch road takes you almost right up to the house.

On presenting a letter of introduction Mr McKinley said he had no room in the house but soon recollected he could give me a shakedown. I was very hungry and had a glorious tea in the kitchen.

Bridgewater is a great fishing station eighteen fisherman (quondam ploughman) residing there who are engaged in the pursuit of a delicious fish known in Queenscliffe as the Trevalla but here called the Haddock.

##### January 22nd. 

Notwithstanding Mr McKinley professed his inability to make me comfortable I could not have been more so. In the morning with the other gentlemen visitors and McKinley I went to see some caves at the extremity of the cape. The first cave is entered by an opening to get to where you have to scramble up a place six feet high and as steep as the side of a house. It is from twenty to thirty yards lone of limestone formation and therefore ornamented with the usual stalactites.

The other cave is like a lofty hall and is so large that you can see it from the Adelaide steamer when passing. To mix the sublime with the ridiculous Mr McKinley gave us a Gaelic song. In the afternoon I went much further round the coast to see some blowholes. Nothing I have seen on my trip can be even compared with the sublime scenery here. It was a calm day when I saw it yet often when the waves thundered against the cliffs a cloud of spray would be sent up far above our heads and we were sitting eighty feet above the water on one of the lowest tiers of the cliff. The Cliffs here are of a dark blue color in formation and general appearance something like the pictures of the Giant\'s Causeway. We saw two Blowholes. Immediately after a

##### January 23rd. 

Left about 9.30 A.M. for some lakes near the coast on the Western side of Cape Bridgewater. A walk of six miles over hills and sand brought me to them. The rabbits were about in swarms. Some curious caves in the side of a hill are worth seeing. Having said goodbye to McKinley I turned up North till I struck the road which would take me to the mainroad I was on before between Portland and the Punt. This led near the coast for four miles. The man showed me where the Admella was wrecked about fifteen miles to the West of Cape Northumberland and told me the following concerning it from memory.

\"The Admella\" was a screw steamer of 300 horsepower flying between Adelaide and Melbourne. It left Adelaide for its last voyage with eighty or ninety souls on board on Friday August th 5th 1859. On the following morning when running before the wind to allow to be shifted \"Barber\" one of the two horses which were on their way to Melbourne to compete in the Champion Race, it struck a reef 15 miles west of Cape Northumberland and immediately broke in three pieces. The number on the poop now was 50 and to support life they had one filter of water, some cheese and almonds, a ham and a few pounds of beef but all of these by some accident got washed overboard on Sunday night and after this the survivors till they were rescued had nothing to eat or drink.

On Monday morning two of the sailors John Leech and Robert Knapman got safely ashore on a raft constructed out of the boom. A.L. Gordon \'s \"Ride from the wreck\" is a description of their journey. The South Australian government send the \'Coris\', a small steamer, with lifeboat and buoys to the assistance of the survivors and it arrived on Wednesday night, but the weather was so rough that nothing could be done. Everyday the number of the people on the poop grew less and less and the spectators on the cliffs could see all that was going on could see some passengers maddended with thirst jump into the sea, could see others wracked by hunger gradually relax their hold and slip into the raging main, boiling trough, jumping across or whatever you like to call it and such cries as these would pass through the crowd. \'Fishers\' in\' there goes Jones, Good God Whittaker\'s done for and etc.

By Tuesday night over twenty died from exposure alone, on other nights the average was four to eight. Mr Magary sliped overboard on Wednesday morning and though he struggled frantically for life it was of no avail. He sank not three hundred yards from the steamer. The \'Ladybird\' a trader between Portland and Melbourne was sent on Wednesday morning by its owners to the wreck. The Victorian Government under Sir John O\'Shaunessy having refused to send any assistance. The Ladybird arrived midday on Friday having with it a Portland whaleboat. Captain Greig in the latter and Captain Fawthroy in the lifeboat went so near th wreck that they could hear piercing cries for Help! Water! They shouted in reply Cheer Up! Back in the morning!

On Friday morning twenty seven people were still alive but four poor fellows when almost in the arms of safety died during the night. The remaining twenty three were all safely got off on Saturday August 13th, 19 by the boats from the steamers and four by a boat from the land. Among those saved was one lady a Miss Ledmouth.

She recounted the whole of her rows with an unsteady plasterer the amusing hunt she had after her eldest son who had run away from a hot tempered father etc. She told me she sometimes took in boarders so I resolved to stop with her. See us at tea. Mrs Roberts at the head of the table helping the salt mutton, self at the other end pouring out the tea. Poor Mrs Roberts she had no control whatever over her h\'s and slung them about in the most liberal manner. She was determined however to appear a real lady in the presence of a poor wretch on the wallaby and though some times she forgot herself and put the tablecloth to other uses than it was intended for, did she not make up for it by the charming grace which she infused into the request to\' and \'sr some of them scones?

##### February 5th 

Was again pleasantly cool. I walked today sixteen miles to Coolatoo. There was but little variety in the scenery. The grassy flats became chains of white lakes perfectly dry and in many places covered with low stunted bushes. This was the commencement of the Coorong proper. At the other side of the lakes is an immense tract of country covered with the Mallee shrub. The road I was on is the mail road between Meningie and Kingston and is used by two four horsed coaches every day. There is a telegraph line and mile posts. Before getting to Coolatoo the sandhills retreated to the left and the road lies across grassy flats. Near the 26th milepost some new coach stables were being put up. There is an excellent hotel at Coolatoo where the coach calls to allow the passengers to get some tea. It is kept by a Mr Robson who couldn\'t have made me more comfortable.

##### February 6th 

Left about 10.30. After six miles of the same sort of walking I had yesterday I came to the Mr Gall\'s outstation Cantara where I got some water. The road which previously had been on the side of the Coorong next the sea now crossed over and for the rest of the journey it was on the other side. Five miles from Cantara I came to some more Coach Stables Chinamen\'s bells almost buried in the sand. How anybody could live there is incomprehensible. Five miles further on and I came to water fully a mile out in the centre of the pipeclay six miles more three down the pipeclay and three through the mallee scrub and I crossed the saltcreek, which runs into the Coorong, by a substantial bridge and the Salk Creek accommodation house is on the slope of the hill to my right, From it I got a fine view of the Coorong with the sand hummocks on the other side. Grassy slopes are in front of the house mallee scrub on both sides and behind. Mr McLeod is a fine handsome man the very image of Don Quixoto.

This house is notorious as once being the publichouse kept by the murderer Malachi Martin. This gentleman went out riding one day with a Mr Robertson. The two quarrelled and the former hit the latter over the head with a loaded stockwhip handle and then placed him on the ground with his throat cut and a penknife in his hand so as to make believe he had committed suicide. Malachi married his widow. A jewellery hawker travelling this way was missed, some of his wares were found on Mrs Martin and his empty box was discovered under the Salt Creek Bridge. The parents of the girl he has as a general servant sent for her to come home to Adelaide. She was owed two years wages and to save this Malachi murdered her and the then stuffed the corpse down a wombat hole. The crows betrayed the deed, she was found by the blacks and Mr Martin expiated his crime. Mrs Martin is now remarried and keeps a shop in Kingston. They say that a traveller walking along the road on a dark windy night is followed by a huge black cat the very appearance of which raises an involuntary shudder which meows piteously walking in a mysterious manner in his footsteps as if it had some dreadful bloodcurdling secret to divulge. Mr McLeod made my hair stand on end narrating this.

##### February 7th 

Would have slept well last night but for that horrid blackcat. Today was so frightfully hot that I thought it advisable to rest during the heat of the day. At 4.30 P.M. I set off for a short walk. There was a good road nearly all the way. Three miles from Salt Creek I came across an encampment of blacks and half casts and a mile or two further on there were a lot more. Just before getting to the Mailstables at Policeman\'s Point I had a fine stretch of beach to cross. To take the stiffness out of my legs I crossed this at a run. Policeman\'s Point is six miles from Salt Creek. A litte way beyond stationery in the sand was a huge American Van filled with the members and effects of some American Panorama Company. They had driven overland from Adelaide and were advertised to play in Kingston that night. The house at Woods\' Wells my destination is distant six miles from Policeman\'s Point and is situated on a bare peninsula running far into the Coorong. The whole walk was close to the sea and the number of teal I saw was simply incredible. They rose at my approach in dense black clouds and this was for several miles. Robson & his wife keep the house and they made me most comfortable.

##### February 8th 

Sunday. Though loth to leave Robson who as an old sailor entertained me greatly, stern necessity demanded that I should start early so I got away by 7 A.M. My road still lay close to the water\'s edge. The Coorong is studded with beautiful islands, some barren and rugged, others and of these the majority, fertile and regular. Low sharp ledges of rocks run out into the sea enclosing sheltered little bays and in every one of these there were lovely mountain ducks. The teal were also in abundance swimming about further out. The first house met with is the mailstable at Cocoanut Wells eight miles from Woods\' Wells and the solitary occupant rather surprised me by telling that that a man can wade across the Coorong to the other side a mile and a half off.

After leaving the stables the track cuts off an immense peninsular and the tourist loses sight of the Coorong and dosen\'t see it again till he gets to McGrath\'s Flat. A hard metal road brought me to the latter place, four miles from Cocoanut Wells. I retain a vivid recollection of the stew I had for dinner. It was composed of the remains, chiefly the necks of all the teal that had been eaten in the house in the last day or two and down here they eat nothing else. McGraths flat gets its name from the singular circumstances that once many years ago a man named McGrath was murdered somewhere near here by the blacks.

The day was frightfully hot on the low banks of the Coorong below the house were three wurleys or native huts made of old sacks stretched upon sticks and such wretched habitations that none but an Australian native could live in them. The islands about here are very pretty covered with grass and honeysuckles which in the distance gives them a very parklike appearance. I had intended to walk right on into Meningie 14 miles in a stretch but on getting to the Needles Mail stables a distance of only two miles I was so hot and tired that I went in an asked permissison to have a rest. The man in charge an honest looking young German cordially asked me to be seated, declared that the shooting near here was the best in South Australia and invited me to stop and have a day\'s sport with him. It was easily arranged. I went back to McCallum\'s the hotel keeper at McGrath\'s flat and bought two pounds of shot. Mrs Inch at tea showed her well devolped lady like qualities such as spitting in the fireplace engaging in playful little erucating contests with her husband and drinking out of the spout of the teapot.

##### February 9th 

Spent nearly all day out shooting with Inch. All kinds of wildfowl were in multitudes on the Corrong. A good shot could have made a splendid bag. We took up our station on a ledge of rocks running out in the direction of an island half a mile distant. Dummies were placed at intervals across to this island to drive the ~~wild~~ teal ~~to where we~~ within range of our guns. Flock after flock flew swiftly past us during the whole afternoon.

##### February 10th 

Was a fine day for travelling - nice and cloudy. I got away at 10 A.M. Dense Mallee scrub was on both sides of the road for some miles. You lose sight of the Coorong on leaving the Needles if indeed it could be seen from the needles. The road a pipeclay was the best for a pedestrian that I have yet seen on my trip. The country gradually opened as I went along, uninteresting forest taking the place of the rich coloured mallee. From the hill near Meningie you see Lake Albert reaching to the horizon, the township consisting of a few scattered houses on both sides of the mainroad lies at your feet. The water of Lake Albert tasted delicious after the hard water I had been drinking for the last three weeks. I saw down on the end of the jetty, where the steamer from Milang lands her passengers, and had some lunch.

For six miles after leaving Meningie my course was due north beside lake Albert over perfectly flat country. Four blacks I met three men and a woman, the former in front the woman a blackbearded thin legged hag behind carrying and immense quantity of blankets rugs etc. At the Wiltowa swamp which I crossed by a made piece of road I saw an almost incredible numbers of wildfowl. The water was just black with teal from within thirty yards of the road to as far as the eye could reach. I saw also an immense number of native companions. I followed the telegraph line for two miles further ~~and~~ through marshy country covered with stunted bushes and then I saw the house I was bound for half a mile off the track to the right. It was an accommodation house kept by a Mr Yelland and I did fare sumptiously. Bullocks liver and mashed potatoes for tea with delicious bread and butter and only charged 1/-. Mr Yelland was telling me that in a boat accident on the Lake Alexandria some time last year he lost a brother, two sisters and a cousin.

##### February 11th 

Fearfully hot. Washed some socks in the morning so couldn\'t get away till 10 A.M. for the first five miles my walk was through the bush still in a northerly direction to cut off the triangular piece of land which separated Lakes Albert and Alexandria. I was on high ground when I first saw the water but then the road rapidly got lower and for two miles till I got to a farmhouse I was walking over undulating grassy plains. She was a rum old fish the woman from whom I got lunch and couldn\'t say a sentence without begining and making it with the words \"you know\". I started away in such a hurry that I forgot my waterbag and didn\'t discover my loss till I had gone a mile.

However sooner than go without it I lay down my knapsack alongside a telegraph pole and ran back for it yes ran it was so delightful to be unburdened. It was a dreary tramp the thirteen miles into Wellington, firstly through marshy tracks, then over grassy flats, then by a heavy sandy road, then round the lake, through the township and by a made road across the swamp to the Punt. The charge for being ferried across is one penny. The Wellington hotel is an instance of a house built on the sand, in fact it is half buried by that article. The Murray I was told is 100 yards wide at the mouth.

##### February 12th 

Wellington is 65 miles from Adelaide and I hoped to do the distance in two or three days. As there is constant mail communication between the two places there is of course a metal road.

Ten miles wretchedly uninteresting travelling over flats and by the northern extremity of Lake Alexandra brought me to a publichouse at Mulgondawa kept by a Dave Dasnke by name. All Danes are surly this man was especially so. Ten miles further on I passed through Langhorne\'s Bridge township and remembers being struck with a shop kept by Berry and Smith and after yet another ten miles a geat part of which was walked in the dark I got into Strathalbyn at 10 P.M. and made for the Victoria Hotel kept by a widow Mrs \_\_\_. She was in a great state of anxiety about a little girl which had gone astray managed to give me a good tea.

##### February 13th 

The church bells were rung last night and every male person in Strathalbyn turned out to look for the child. They thoroughly searched the Angas River on which the town is situated groped into every corner and look into every well in the place.

About 9 A.M. this morning a horseman came in with the news that the child had turned up safe at Port Victor. It had been playing about and fallen to sleep in the van, was unnoticed by the driver and hence the catastrophe.

Strathalbyn is a very picturesque town with handsome churches and hotels. The newspaper is the Southern Argus and there is a population of about fifteen hundred. I had a glorious walk though the sun was very powerful through country thickly timbered on both sides of the road, Macclesfield is beautifully situated eight miles from Strathalbyn. Six miles farther on is Echunga celebrated for the goldmines in the vicinity also in a glorious position. I had my dinner at the Hagen Arms. I could only get eight miles nearer Adelaide I was so tired so I stopped at the Aldgate Pump where Mr Myers has an excellent Hotel. He had some discriminative power and treated me like a gentleman even though I was but a tramp.

##### February 14th 

Saturday. Today I completed my walk I wished to get into Adelaide by 12 oclock so I left at 8 A.M.

Being in good condition I frequently ran. The scenery is of course superb and is too well known for me to spoil by attempting to describe it. I was in ecstasies all the way in fact from Strathalbyn the walk has been almost equal to the sublime scenery in the Cape Otway forest.

I got some lime juice at the celebrated \"Eagle on the Hill\" and arrived in Adelaide at the Post Office at three minutes before 12. My whole walk had been 652 miles or the way I went 752. I loafed about town for an hour or two and in the afternoon went down to the oval to see Jarvis play. As I seated myself in the grandstand a general titter passed through the crowd and everybody tried to be funny at my expense. Just because I had on a flannel cricketing shirt perhaps a little dirty old serge breeches, a green cap, leggings, old boots and a knapsack. One pertinacious old fool was very inquisitive and would have that I had been down two years before with sheep and that he had seen me.

I have now finished with my walk and my diary and I certainly have found the writing of the latter the more arduous of the two. I am fairly done up with exhaustion so I\'ll shut up.

By the way Jarvis got run out first ball.

The End
